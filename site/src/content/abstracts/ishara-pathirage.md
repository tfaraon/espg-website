Rock varnish is a Mn-rich coating that develops on weathered rock surfaces, yet the relationship between varnish chemistry, the underlying weathered substrate and fresh host rock remains poorly resolved. An investigation has been conducted examining the mineralogical and geochemical changes from fresh gabbro through the weathered zone to rock varnish on Rosemary Island gabbro, Murujuga, Western Australia, using integrated petrographic, mineralogical and in situ geochemical data from four rock profiles. The gabbro preserves alteration assemblages associated with pre-existing low-grade metamorphism. Surface weathering overprints this, producing a heterogeneous substrate characterised by the breakdown of plagioclase- and clinopyroxene-rich domains, increased porosity, and decreasing Na-, Ca-, and Mg-bearing components toward the surface. Varnish is distinguished by strong Mn accumulation, with median MnO contents approximately 39-70 times the corresponding fresh whole-rock concentrations. Also concentrated in the varnish are P2O5, Co, Ba, Cu, and Rb, whereas Fe remains more evenly distributed through both the coating and underlying weathered gabbro. Although the varnish composition generally appears to be independent of both the whole-rock MgO content and weathering-rind thickness, correlations with MnO, Ba and Zr suggest there may be element-specific associations with host-rock composition. The varnish compositions vary in detail but are typically LREE-enriched and show positive Ce anomalies (Ce/Ce\* = 2.2-7.6) and negative Eu anomalies (Eu/Eu\* = 0.6-0.7). These results are consistent with data published in other studies and indicate that varnish development involves the incorporation of wind-blown dust and the selective accumulation of elements at the weathered rock surface
