Sub-daily precipitation extremes threaten urban environments as the primary driver of flash flooding events. There is emerging observational evidence showing that these extremes have been increasing in frequency and intensity at rates greater than theoretical atmospheric thermodynamics would predict with increasing global temperatures. Currently, this result remains spatially and temporally heterogeneous, with little certainty about underlying drivers.

Due to the susceptibility of densely populated environments to the worst impacts of flash floods, it is important to identify when, where, and why this trend is being observed. This study provides this clarity for the southeastern coast of Australia by considering varying seasonal and diurnal trends in observational hourly rainfall data as well as characterising changes in underlying convective environments using high-resolution BARRA-C2 model data over a 25 year period. Results show that while there is moderate intensification of hourly rainfall extremes, the signal is temporally variable, as most significant increases in both the frequency and intensity of extremes are identified for events within 2pm–8pm of Austral Summer. Ocean warming influencing enhanced atmospheric moisture and convection over the southeastern coastline is implicated as a likely driver, shown by spatial coherence between hourly rainfall intensification, increasing frequency of convective conditions, warming Tasman Sea waters, and enhanced specific humidity.

This study exemplifies the importance of considering local-scale mechanisms and conducting hourly resolved analysis in future research – both contributing to the regional understanding of hourly rainfall extremes and underscoring the need to prepare both communities and infrastructure for the likelihood of increasing impacts of sub-daily rainfall extremes as we enter a warmer world.
