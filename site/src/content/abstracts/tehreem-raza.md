Human activities have long been influenced by several environmental factors, particularly variations in freshwater habitats, coastal dynamics and ecosystem productivity. In southeastern Australia, fluctuations in sea level, freshwater influx and sea surface temperature during the Holocene influenced the structure of estuarine and coastal habitats which sustained both marine ecosystems and human activities. Understanding these long-term environmental fluctuations is essential for interpreting the patterns of settlement, habitat maintenance as well as resource management. Archaeological shell midden sites in Western Victoria, particularly those within Gunditjmara Country, provide a unique archive of these interactions, preserving not only the evidence of shellfish harvesting but also the chronological, geochemical and archaeological records of past environmental conditions. Mollusc shells are valuable biogeochemical archives of past environmental conditions. Through Sclerochronology—the study of physical and chemical variations in hard tissues—stable isotope (e.g., δ¹⁸O, δ¹³C) and trace element analyses (e.g., Mg/Ca, Sr/Ca, Ba/Ca) can be used to reconstruct the environmental variables such as water temperature, salinity and upwelling, to resolve seasonal patterns at high temporal resolution. This project will apply high-resolution sclerochronological techniques to modern and archaeological mollusc shells to reconstruct past environmental conditions and seasonal Indigenous foraging practices in Western Victoria. A multi-analytical approach will be employed, including Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS), isotope ratio mass spectrometry and radiocarbon dating, Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectrometry (ICP-AES) trace element analysis and Raman spectroscopy. These complementary techniques will enable precise elemental and isotopic characterisation, chronological records and robust environmental proxy development and application. This research comprises two interconnected objectives. First, to collect and analyse modern mollusc shells (limpets and pipis) from key habitats in Western Victoria to calibrate the relationship between shell geochemistry and environmental parameters. This calibration work is crucial for the second objective of interpreting past climate and environmental records from archaeological materials. The calibrated geochemical proxies will be applied to mollusc shells recovered from archaeological shell midden sites within Gunditjmara Country.  These sites hold detailed records of human-environment interactions over millennia. The sclerochronological analyses will allow the reconstruction of seasonal patterns of shellfish harvesting and environmental changes across the Holocene, shedding light on long-term Indigenous resource management practices and adaptations to changing environmental conditions. By integrating geochemical, chronological and archaeological data, this study will advance understanding of long-term human–environment interactions and provide new insights into Indigenous resource management strategies in southeastern Australia.
