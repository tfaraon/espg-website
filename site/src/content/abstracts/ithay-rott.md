Orogenic gold deposits make up the largest source of mined gold in history, yet the mechanisms which concentrate gold from its crustal background concentration (\~0.5–5 ppb) to ore-grade deposits (\>10 ppm) remain poorly constrained. According to the metamorphic devolatilisation model, gold and sulfur are released into hydrothermal fluids through the breakdown of pyrite (FeS2) to pyrrhotite (Fe1-xS), a reaction that has previously been confined to the greenschist–amphibolite transition (\~500–550 °C). This has restricted current models of orogenic gold formation to a deep, narrow source region, despite field evidence of pyrrhotite replacing pyrite at shallower, lower-greenschist conditions.

This study experimentally assessed whether applied differential stress can drive the pyrite-pyrrhotite transition below its conventionally accepted temperature threshold. Pyrite cubes were reacted with graphite, chlorite, muscovite or biotite at 250–350 °C, under differential stresses of 50–500 bar applied via a titanium tapered-puck loading system, with paired unstressed experiments run under identical conditions. At 250 °C, no pyrrhotite formed regardless of applied stress. At 350 °C, however, samples reacted under differential stress exhibited substantial pyrrhotite crystallisation, associated microcracking, and significantly elevated mass loss (\~5%, compared to \~0.09% in unstressed controls), while unstressed samples at the same temperature showed no reaction at all. As little as 50 bar of applied stress was sufficient to initiate the transition, and the extent of pyrrhotite replacement increased with reaction time, with samples reacted for two weeks fully converted.

These results demonstrate that differential stress can extend the pyrite-pyrrhotite transition, and therefore the liberation of sulfur and gold, down to lower greenschist facies conditions previously considered too cool for this reaction to proceed. As differential stress and fluid flow are both concentrated along the same structural pathways that transport hydrothermal fluids, this finding supports a revised model in which structural pathways act not merely as conduits but as additional, shallower sources of sulfur and gold, progressively enriching ascending fluids through repeated seismic cycling. This substantially widens the region from which sulfur and gold can be sourced in the formation of orogenic gold deposits, and highlights differential stress as a previously overlooked control on their formation.
