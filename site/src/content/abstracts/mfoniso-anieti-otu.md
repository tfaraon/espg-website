The Earth’s magnetic field is invisible, but we can track its behavior from the ability some magnetic particles within sediments have to align with the field. One example is detrital magnetic particles settling on the ocean floor behaving like tiny compass needles and effectively recording geomagnetic field history through time. However, these records are rare in the Southern Hemisphere, leading to geographical bias in global geomagnetic field models. Increasing the geographical coverage of paleomagnetic records is therefore important for constraining Southern Hemisphere geomagnetic variability.

Edisto Inlet, a fjord in the western Ross Sea, provides an unusual opportunity to address this gap. Two 14.5-m-long, finely laminated marine sediment cores, HLF17-01 and TR17-08, were recovered only 0.6 km apart on a sediment drift that accumulates sediments ultra-rapidly (\~600 cm/ka). The cores have overlapping Late Holocene chronologies (last 2,000 years) and include the Mt Rittmann tephra (687 ± 7 cal BP) independently dated from the WAIS Divide ice-core chronology. The cores’ proximity is particularly valuable because the paleomagnetic signal recorded by an individual core is rarely unbiased. This is a major problem in Antarctica, where the few available Holocene records are low resolution (\<10 cm/ka), do not compare well and appear to be frequently affected by inclination shallowing. Replication of two high-resolution palaeomagnetic records with independent chronologies will test whether they are telling the same magnetic story.

Here, we present preliminary results comparing both cores’ magnetic susceptibility (MS) and natural remanent magnetization (NRM) on recalibrated chronological constraints from the literature. These comparisons show intervals of broadly similar variability across the overlapping chronology, alongside core-specific differences and possible age offsets. The preliminary age-depth models indicate differences in sedimentation rates and intervals with poor age constraints. This chronological framework will be strengthened with ten additional AMS radiocarbon measurements. Nevertheless, paleomagnetic directions (inclination and declination) were calculated using principal component analysis of the characteristic remanent magnetisation and the results are presented on the preliminary age models. We will discuss how well the two high-resolution records compare together, as well as with the existing low resolution Holocene records from Antarctica and geomagnetic field models.

If reliable recording and replication are established, these neighboring Edisto Inlet archives could provide two closely replicated Late-Holocene palaeomagnetic records from a part of the Southern Hemisphere where independently constrained observations remain scarce, providing much-needed Southern Hemisphere constraints for future geomagnetic field reconstructions and models
