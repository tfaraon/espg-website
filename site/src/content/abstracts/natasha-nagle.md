The Dead Sea, one of the world’s most unique environments, is shrinking at an alarming rate of over 1m/year, with profound consequences for local ecosystems and communities (Ghazleh et al. 2011). This rapid transformation highlights the sensitivity of the Jordan Valley to hydrological and environmental change, but the relatively short period of direct observation provides only a limited perspective on the range, persistence, and effects of such changes. The Valley’s Pleistocene sedimentary record offers a much longer perspective.

Throughout the Pleistocene and Holocene, the Jordan Valley is understood to have hosted a number of lacustrine environments whose deposits preserve a valuable record of Levantine Quaternary environmental change (Torfstein et al. 2009; Waldmann et al. 2009, etc.); recording fluctuations in lake conditions in response to regional climatic and hydrological variability. Rather than representing a simple record of progressive climatic change through time, the Valley’s lacustrine deposits reveal a more complex history of environmental responses (ex: Waldmann et al. 2007, Ben Dor et al. 2019).

Analysis of published materials often reveal significant differences in sediment descriptions and classifications, along with several previously unresolved depositional transitions with important implications for interpreting lake-levels and environmental variability. This study presents new stratigraphic and sedimentological analyses from the first systematic survey and sampling of the Eastern Jordan Valley’s lacustrine deposits, including eighteen previously unidentified Pleistocene and Holocene outcrops, to further clarify our understanding of the Valley’s lacustrine systems and help fill the remaining gaps.

Through the use of detailed examinations of lithofacies (geological description, elemental composition, mineralogy, grain size, and magnetic susceptibility), depositional contacts, and stratigraphic relationships, a series of key features have been identified which point to the existence of a much longer-lasting and more consistent environmental presence within the Valley than previously anticipated. These discoveries provide new constraints on the evolution of the lake systems, and raise important questions regarding the timing, duration, and environmental significance of individual phases of lake development.

By considering the environmental record beyond the modern Dead Sea, these additional contexts provide a longer-term perspective on the sensitivity and resilience of the Jordan Valley’s lacustrine systems, offering critical context for interpreting and anticipating the environmental consequences of continued change today.
