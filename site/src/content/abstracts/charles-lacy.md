Kimberlites and lamproites are rare, deep, volcanic rocks that provide key information on the geochemical and physical attributes of the mantle. These rocks originate as magma in the mantle (likely the asthenosphere) and ascend rapidly up to the surface collecting, and interacting with, surrounding rocks, minerals, and fluids along the way. This complex journey alters the original chemistry of the magma and results in a rock comprised of many different components, making it difficult to decipher the specific source and processes that occurred. This project investigates the “Black and White Wolf” occurrences from Kuusamo, Finland. The Wolves were erupted through an ancient, stable, cratonic continental crust which is considered a key factor in producing this type of volcanic activity. These bodies have been investigated using a combination of microscopic observations and geochemical analysis of the major and trace elements compositions of key mineral phases. In order to understand the magma’s chemical evolution, petrographic and chemical tools are used to differentiate between the foreign material collected during magma ascent and the material that has crystallised from the magma itself. This project aims to refine our interpretation of how these mantle rocks form and evolve by comparing the “Black and White Wolf” samples with other mantle-derived rocks in the Finland region. These new data are also compared with global studies of kimberlite and lamproite occurrences, helping to reduce the gap in scientific knowledge of these complex rocks. Future work will employ age-dating techniques to determine when these samples reached the surface and how they fit into the ‘kimberlitic’ and ‘lamproitic’ activity of the region.
