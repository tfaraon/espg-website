Clean labs (CLs) cleanliness varies according to specific classification and appropriately to experiment conducted. Regardless of these conditions, samples are still exposed to contamination, often small, but not insignificant. With space exploration and return sample missions, materials brought from extraterrestrial bodies are in limited mass and require strictly curated environment and protocols to ensure relevant studies. While analyses tend to target small samples, their sensitivity to contamination is drastically increased.

Towards this objective, this project is evaluating elemental contaminations in CLs, brought from Personal Protective Equipment (PPE; gloves, hairnet, masks, etc.) and other consumables (parafilms, wipes, vials, etc.), when sample are handled and stored within clean rooms (CRs).

Experiments and analyses were carried out on samples from multiple facilities (MIA, Australia; Extraterrestrial Sample Curation Center, Japan) and compared to previous analyses on similar and different materials.

Contaminant sampling is done by dipping a 1cm^2 piece of the sample in a tube filled with 10 mL of 2% HNO3 (0.32M nitric acid) for \~1s, before closing and leaving tubes to equilibrate for a day. In the case of gloves, tests were conducted with raw gloves and other rinsed with ultra-pure water and flicked before the test. Aliquots of 5 mL from main the solution are taken and measured using an Agilent 7900 ICP-QQQ. All other materials used (tubes, caps, tweezer, etc.) were leached prior to experiment.

While being efficiently protective when handling acids, Nitrile and Vinyl gloves do bring contaminants to samples when accidental touch occur, with 50 – 110 ng of Zn (Nitrile) and \~10 ng (Vinyl). Na, Mg, Al, K, Ca, Ti, Fe, Ni, Zn and W contents are significant in all gloves and some wipes. Parafilm must be used carefully and for specific samples as contents in Na, Al, Ca higher than 10 ng were detected. Masks brought non-negligeable Ca (\~200ng), as hairnet (Ca \> 1000ng) with other elements \~1 ng or less (when detected).

Washing gloves prior to tests is effective but need to be cautiously achieved and possibly improved by using acids and reducing touch with equipment as much as possible. After comparative analyses, Nitrile gloves bring more contamination than Vinyl gloves by and order of magnitude (Na, Mg, Al, Ca, K, Fe Zn, etc.). Manufacturing processes as the use of Zn catalysts to counter rubber in gloves explains high contents in these elements (Ca, Sr and Mg notably).

Rinsing gloves is efficient in reducing potential contamination.
