Coastal landforms occur along the margins of many shallow lakes, including lakes in arid regions. Their morphology may preserve evidence of past water levels and wave conditions, but interpreting that evidence is difficult where direct observations are sparse or the conditions under which landforms formed no longer exist. Kati Thanda–Lake Eyre, an ephemeral lake in central Australia, provides an opportunity to investigate these relationships across a range of inundation states.

We use coupled hydrodynamic and wave modelling to explore how wind direction, wind speed and water level control wave generation, circulation and bed shear stress across the lake. By comparing modelled patterns with satellite observations and mapped littoral landforms, we assess which combinations of forcing could form or rework features such as spits, bars and beach ridges. We also examine whether similar landforms can form under different conditions, a source of uncertainty when using them to identify past environments.

This process-based approach offers a way to test the environmental interpretations of coastal landforms in places where monitoring is limited. For Kati Thanda–Lake Eyre, it may help distinguish the roles of changing water level and wind-driven sediment transport, and clarify what these landforms can reveal about past lake and climate conditions.
