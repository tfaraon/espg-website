Uranium (U) mine waste poses significant risks to human health, native biodiversity and groundwater systems. New studies emphasise the ability of bacteria to remediate such mine waste, a method known as bioremediation. U waste is also often enriched in REEs, which are increasingly sought after in the global race for clean energy. Not only can microbes ‘clean up’ toxic elements such as U, but they also provide a way to leach REEs from lowly concentrated tailings. Importantly, the natural bacterial mechanism by which this occurs is phosphate scavenging. Bacteria need P to grow and survive and, as such, have adapted the ability to mobilise it from insoluble P minerals in environments containing limited nutrients. Here, we exploit the natural ability of microbes to mobilise P, in hopes to leach REEs and gain a deeper understanding of the mechanisms for U tolerance. This study cultures U-tolerant bacteria from tailings waste and selects for the most widely applicable organisms: phosphate mobilising bacteria. U-tolerant bacteria were successfully enriched in vitro and a leaching experiment was performed to examine the phosphate solubilising mechanisms. Bacterial community analysis, from DNA sequence data, was also obtained for four timepoints during the leaching experiment. This experiment poses extensive implications for upscaling bioremediation on mine sites, and for understanding the natural mechanism of U-tolerant bacteria in the environment. The findings here are key for not only U waste, but also for the bioremediation of all heavy elements.
