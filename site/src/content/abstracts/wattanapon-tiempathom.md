Aerosols play an important role in climate and air quality, influencing the Earth’s radiation balance, cloud formation, and human health. Their impacts depend not only on aerosol abundance but also on their vertical distribution. The combination of local emissions, transported pollution, and atmospheric processes can result in complex aerosol vertical distributions. In particular, long-range transport and biomass burning can also contribute to the formation of elevated aerosol layers (EALs). Understanding EALs can provide valuable insights into aerosol vertical structure and support air pollution modelling and forecasting, as well as the interpretation of satellite aerosol observations. However, the occurrence, characteristics, and potential drivers of these layers over Melbourne remain poorly understood.

This study uses ground-based Multi-Axis Differential Optical Absorption Spectroscopy (MAX-DOAS), a remote-sensing technique that measures scattered sunlight at multiple viewing angles. Absorption measurements of the oxygen collision complex (O₂–O₂, or O₄) are used to infer changes in light path due to aerosol scattering and retrieve vertical profiles of aerosol extinction. Observations over Melbourne from January 2024 to April 2026 across three viewing azimuths (0°, 85°, and 240°) were analysed to characterise EALs. For this study, EALs were identified between 1 and 4 km altitude using thresholds for aerosol extinction and peak prominence, with confirmed events requiring detection in at least three consecutive profiles. Their occurrence, altitude, thickness, duration, and associations with meteorological conditions were examined. Backward air-mass trajectories and satellite fire-hotspot observations were also used to investigate potential transport and biomass-burning influences.

EALs were detected on 91% of valid observing days in summer and 88% in spring, compared with 78% in autumn and 69% in winter (Figure 1a). Despite this seasonal variation in occurrence, their vertical characteristics were relatively consistent, with layers typically centred at approximately 1.5–1.7 km and having a typical thickness of 0.55–0.60 km (Figures 1b–c). Events typically persisted for approximately 4–5 h, with longer durations generally observed in summer and spring (Figure 1d). Relatively high conditional occurrence under west to south-westerly winds suggests a possible influence of regional aerosol transport. A case study during 24–26 January 2026 showed that several backward trajectories associated with EALs passed over active-fire regions, suggesting a possible transported biomass-burning contribution.

These findings show that EALs are a frequent feature of Melbourne’s lower troposphere, particularly during summer and spring, rather than isolated episodic features. Their occurrence and persistence appear to be associated with a combination of meteorological conditions, regional transport, and episodic biomass-burning influence. Characterising these layers provides a more complete picture of Melbourne’s aerosol environment than can be obtained from near-surface observations alone.
