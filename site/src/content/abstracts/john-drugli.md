Thunderstorm Asthma (TA) is a major public health burden placing sudden and severe strain on hospitals and local health authorities. In Victoria, Australia, previous research suggests that TA is triggered by boundary layer convergence lines (CL). However, not all CLs occurring during the TA season will trigger an event, and the exact meteorological conditions distinguishing TA-triggering from non-TA-triggering CLs remain uncertain. This study uses the Bureau’s Atmospheric Regional Reanalysis for Australia C2 (BARRA-C2), combined with historical pollen observations and hospital admission data for 12 recorded TA events, to investigate the dynamics and thermodynamics of CLs in Victoria and their role in the occurrence of TA. Results show that CLs in the region can be clustered into four main categories based on morphology, orientation, and size, revealing that the most impactful TA events are related to large, linear features while less impactful events are caused by localised, strongly curved CLs. Additionally, the CLs triggering TA tend to be characterised by hot, dry, and windy surface conditions, while all CLs exhibit significant internal variability in along-line cross-sectional profiles of potential temperature, humidity, low-level horizontal vorticity, and flow trajectories. This suggests that the conditions necessary for TA may occur on a sub-CL scale, potentially contributing to the explanation of why not all CLs occurring during the TA season trigger an event. Overall, these findings provide increased understanding of the structure of TA-triggering CLs, which could help inform future early warning systems.
