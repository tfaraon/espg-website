While ventilation is a key parameter affecting indoor environmental quality and the energy consumption of buildings, standard ventilation guidelines typically specify prescriptive airflow rates that apply uniformly across room types, regardless of how a space is actually used; this can leave occupants under-protected in some settings while wasting energy in others. This work presents an alternative design framework, grounded in a modified Wells–Riley infection-risk model, that calculates the outdoor airflow a room needs based on its specific use conditions — including volume, occupant count, seating density, occupied duration, and supplementary controls such as face masks or portable air purification. The approach was tested in two university spaces, where distinct usage scenarios were modelled to reflect realistic combinations of occupancy and behaviour. Each mitigation measure was evaluated individually for its capacity to offset additional outdoor air requirements: reduced duration (8 hr to 5 hr) and portable air purification lowered the required rate by nearly two-fifths and one-fifth, while mask-wearing and increased spacing between occupants (0.5 m to 1 m) achieved reductions of nearly half and one-quarter, respectively. When layered together, these measures cut overall ventilation demand by up to two-thirds relative to a no-intervention baseline. These findings suggest that pairing behavioural and technological interventions with a risk-based ventilation target offers a practical route to substantial energy savings, without asking building operators to trade away occupant health to achieve them.
